\title{Relationship Between Local Radiation and Temperature}
\author{Dustin Randall}
\date{}
\maketitle

It is a common assertion that the average temperature in the world has been increasing over the past several decades.~\citationneeded{}
One potential hypothesis to explain this trend is that the sun has also been increasing in its temperature over the same period.
What does the Spokane weather data tell us about the effect of radiation on daily temperature?
To answer this question, we look at the weather data from 1970 through 2025.

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/annual_weather_1.png}
  \caption{Total radiation and temperature stats over 55 years}
  \label{fig:annual_weather}
\end{figure}

Figure 1 shows two opposing trends: the total radiation received over each year has decreased, while the minimum, average, and maximum temperatures have steadily increased.
According to these data, the increase in temperature does not appear to directly correlate with the total radiation received.

Another idea that people have proposed is that weather has become more extreme over time.~\citationneeded{}
In order to define extreme weather, we must first establish a baseline of typical weather.
For this paper, we take a 30-year span from 1/1/1985 through 12/31/2014.
The particular choice of years is largely arbitrary, so I chose the year I was born as the starting point.
Using this baseline, I define extreme weather as any day where the observed data point is more than one standard deviation outside typical.

More specifically, let $m$ denote a calendar month and $v$ a measured variable.
Let $x_{i,m,v}$ be the $i$th valid daily observation of value $v$ in month $m$ during the 1985--2014 baseline.
Let $n_{m,v}$ be the number of these observations.
For a day $d$ in month $m$, let $x_{d,v}$ be the observed daily value of variable $v$.

\[
\mu_{m,v} = \frac{1}{n_{m,v}} \sum_{i=1}^{n_{m,v}} x_{i,m,v}
\]
\[
s_{m,v} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n_{m,v}-1} \sum_{i=1}^{n_{m,v}}{(x_{i,m,v}-\mu_{m,v})^2}}
\]
\[
z_{d,v} = \frac{x_{d,v} - \mu_{m,v}}{s_{m,v}}
\]

I classify a day as extreme based on the z-score being more than one standard deviation away from the mean.

\[
\text{label} = \begin{cases}
  hot \text{ or } bright & \text{if } z_{d,v} > 1 \\
  cold \text{ or } dim & \text{if } z_{d,v} < -1 \\
\end{cases}
\]

  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/outliers_1.png}
  \caption{Annual Temperature And Radiation Outliers}
  \label{fig:outliers_stats}
\end{figure}

Figure 2 shows two plots: the top plot shows the number of unusually hot and cold days per year.
The bottom plot shows the number of unusually bright and dim days per year.
The trend lines on these plots tell us that the number of unusually cold days has been steadily decreasing, while the number of unusually hot days has been increasing.
Counter to what would be expected, the number of unusually bright days has been steadily decreasing over time, and the number of unusually dim days has been increasing.
This provides further evidence that the increase in unusually hot days is not a direct result of increased radiation, at least in Spokane.

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/scatter_2.png}
  \caption{Scatter Plot of Daily Radiation vs Daily High Temperature}
  \label{fig:scatter}
\end{figure}

Figure 3 shows that there is a slight correlation between daily radiation and daily high temperature, with an r value of 0.24.
This makes some sense because seasonal variation in temperature and radiation are correlated with both trending higher in the summer months.
This correlation however does not imply a causal relationship between the two, and may be explained by other factors.

We define $r$ as the Pearson correlation coefficient between daily radiation and daily high temperature.
Let $R_d$ and $T_d$ denote the standardized radiation and high-temperature anomalies for paired day $d$, and let $N$ be the count of valid paired days.
$\bar{R}$ and $\bar{T}$ denote the mean of $R_d$ and $T_d$ respectively.

\[
r =
\frac{\sum_{d=1}^{N}(R_d-\bar{R})(T_d-\bar{T})}
{\sqrt{
  \left[\sum_{d=1}^{N}{(R_d-\bar{R})^2}\right]
  \left[\sum_{d=1}^{N}{(T_d-\bar{T})^2}\right]
}}
\]

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/lag_1.png}
  \caption{Radiation Lead vs Temperature Anomaly Correlation}
  \label{fig:lag}
\end{figure}

Finally, I analyzed whether the radiation received on a given day has a strong predictive effect on the future temperature.
Figure 4 shows that the correlation between radiation and temperature is strongest when radiation leads temperature by 1 day, but the correlation is still relatively weak.
These data suggest that the increase in temperature over the last 55 years in Spokane cannot adequately be explained by changes in daily radiation.
In fact, Spokane's total radiation trended downward over time which may have dampened the effect on the temperature increase.
